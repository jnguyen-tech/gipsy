# Risk and how to learn markets

## This is education, not financial advice
Gipsy is a study buddy. It can explain how markets work, show what an indicator says and fetch prices and news, but it does not know John's full financial situation, cannot predict prices, and should never tell him to buy or sell something. Live data can be delayed or wrong. Any real decision about money deserves a qualified, licensed adviser and John's own judgment. Under 18 in the US, a brokerage account needs a parent or guardian (a custodial account). Investing or trading on someone else's tips, or on social-media hype, is how most beginners lose money.

## Risk first: the one percent idea
Professionals think about how much they could lose before they think about how much they could make. A common rule is to risk no more than about 1% of the account on a single idea. Risk here means the money lost if the stop loss is hit, not the size of the position. Example: with a $1,000 paper account, 1% is $10. If you buy at $50 with a stop at $48, you risk $2 per share, so the position is 5 shares ($250 worth). Position size = money at risk ÷ distance to the stop. This keeps any single mistake small, so you survive long enough to learn.

## Stop losses and reward-to-risk
A stop loss is a pre-set exit if the trade goes wrong. Put it where your idea is proven wrong, for example below the swing low or below the EMA you were relying on, not at a random percentage. Reward-to-risk compares the distance to your target with the distance to your stop. Risking $2 to try to make $6 is 3:1. With 3:1 trades you can be wrong more than half the time and still come out ahead, because winners are bigger than losers. Moving a stop further away because you don't want to be wrong is the classic mistake that turns a small loss into a big one.

## Keep a trading journal
For every paper trade, write down the date, the symbol, the timeframe, why you entered (what you saw on the chart), where the stop and target were, how big the position was, how you felt, and what happened. Screenshot the chart at entry and at exit. Review the journal every week. Patterns jump out: maybe you lose on Mondays, or whenever you trade against the 200 EMA, or when you chase a candle that already moved a lot. The journal turns random experience into real learning.

## Common beginner mistakes
Overtrading: taking trades out of boredom. Chasing: buying after a huge green candle because of fear of missing out. Revenge trading: trying to win back a loss immediately. Too much leverage: futures, options, margin and crypto perpetuals can wipe out an account in a single move. Too many indicators: five indicators that say the same thing are not five confirmations. Ignoring fees, spreads and taxes. Believing a backtest or a guru without checking. Going all-in on one asset. Slow, boring and diversified is how most long-term wealth is actually built, for example regular contributions to a broad index fund.

## A learning path with Gipsy and TradingView
Week 1: learn the TradingView screen, candlesticks and timeframes. Build watchlists for metals, rates, crypto and housing. Week 2: add EMA 20/50/200 to daily charts of SPY, gold and bitcoin, and describe each trend out loud to Gipsy. Week 3: support, resistance and volume. Use Bar Replay to practise 50 decisions. Week 4: put gold and US10Y side by side, then bitcoin and the Nasdaq 100, and use the Compare button to see the relationships in the knowledge pack for yourself. Week 5 onward: paper trade with the 1% rule and keep a journal. Ask Gipsy to quiz you, to explain anything you don't understand, and to pull the latest news when a market moves sharply.
