# How markets connect: gold, silver, bonds, the dollar, crypto and housing

## The big idea: everything is priced against interest rates
Almost every asset competes with the "safe" return you can get from lending to the US government. When Treasury yields are high, cash and bonds pay well, so investors demand more from anything riskier or anything that pays nothing, like gold. When yields fall, the hurdle drops, and money tends to flow toward stocks, gold, crypto and property. That is why markets react so strongly to the Federal Reserve and to inflation data. The relationships below are tendencies, not laws. They hold for long stretches, then break for a year or two when something else dominates.

## Bonds: price and yield move in opposite directions
A bond pays a fixed coupon. Say you hold a 10-year bond paying 3% and new bonds start paying 5%. Nobody will pay full price for your 3% bond, so its price falls until its effective return (its yield) matches the market. So when yields go up, existing bond prices go down, and the other way round. Longer bonds move more for the same yield change. That sensitivity is called duration. TLT, an ETF of 20+ year Treasuries, can drop sharply when long yields rise. On TradingView, US10Y is the 10-year yield and TLT is the bond price. They should look like mirror images.

## What moves yields: the Fed, inflation and growth
The Federal Reserve sets the short-term policy rate (the fed funds rate), which anchors very short yields such as the 3-month bill and heavily influences the 2-year. Longer yields like the 10-year also reflect what investors expect for growth and inflation over many years, plus a term premium for the risk of locking money up for a long time. Hot inflation or strong growth tends to push yields up. Weak growth, falling inflation or a scare in markets tends to pull them down, as investors run to safety. Large government borrowing can also push long yields up, because more bonds need buyers.

## The yield curve
The yield curve plots yields from short to long maturities. Normally longer bonds pay more, so the curve slopes up. When short yields rise above long yields (for example the 2-year above the 10-year), the curve is inverted. That has preceded most US recessions, because it means markets expect the Fed to have to cut rates in future. It is a slow signal: recessions have historically arrived anywhere from months to a couple of years after inversion, and the moment the curve un-inverts (steepens again) has often been closer to the trouble. The FRED series T10Y2Y tracks the 10-year minus 2-year spread.

## Real yields and gold
Gold pays no interest. Its main competitor is a safe bond that pays a return above inflation, which is the real yield (nominal yield minus expected inflation, tracked by 10-year TIPS yields). For many years gold and real yields moved in opposite directions: falling real yields made gold more attractive, rising real yields made it less so. In recent years that link weakened, as central banks, especially in emerging markets, bought large amounts of gold to diversify away from the dollar, and gold rose even while real yields were high. Gold also tends to rise during fear, war and financial stress (a safe-haven bid) and when people worry about currency debasement.

## The US dollar
Gold, silver, oil and most commodities are priced in dollars. When the dollar strengthens, they cost more for buyers using other currencies, which tends to weigh on their dollar price. When the dollar weakens, they tend to rise. The DXY index measures the dollar against a basket led by the euro. A strong dollar often comes from higher US rates than elsewhere, or from global fear, because the dollar is the world's main safe-haven currency. A strong dollar also tightens conditions for the rest of the world and has tended to be a headwind for crypto and emerging markets.

## Silver: half precious metal, half industrial metal
Silver follows gold in the big picture, but more than half of its demand is industrial: electronics, solar panels and other manufacturing. So it also behaves like a growth-sensitive commodity. It is a smaller, less liquid market, so it usually moves more than gold in both directions. People often call it gold with higher volatility. The gold-to-silver ratio (gold price divided by silver price; XAUUSD/XAGUSD on TradingView) shows how many ounces of silver buy one ounce of gold. Historically it has spent much of its time roughly between 40 and 90. A very high ratio has often come with fear or recession worries, when gold does better. A falling ratio often comes with strong growth and metals rallies, when silver catches up.

## Crypto as a risk asset and as digital gold
Bitcoin's supporters call it digital gold: fixed supply (21 million coins), not controlled by a government. In practice, most of the time bitcoin has traded like a high-risk technology asset. It tends to rise when money is easy (falling rates, a weak dollar, central banks adding liquidity) and to fall hard when conditions tighten, as in 2022 when rates rose fast. It has often been positively correlated with the Nasdaq. Smaller coins (altcoins) usually move more than bitcoin both ways. Bitcoin's four-year halving cycle, when the new-coin reward per block halves, is a popular story for its booms and busts, though the effect is debated. Spot bitcoin ETFs in the US (since 2024) tied it more closely to traditional market flows.

## Housing and mortgage rates
In the US, 30-year mortgage rates follow the 10-year Treasury yield plus a spread (historically around 1.5 to 2 percentage points, wider in stressed times). When the 10-year rises, mortgages get more expensive quickly. That changes what buyers can afford: on a $400,000 loan, the monthly principal and interest is about $2,400 at 6% and about $1,900 at 4%. Higher rates usually slow sales first. Prices react slowly, because owners with cheap old mortgages don't want to sell (the lock-in effect), which keeps supply low. So housing can see falling sales and still-high prices at the same time. Homebuilder stocks (XHB, ITB) and REITs (VNQ) are rate-sensitive and often move ahead of the actual housing data. FRED has the weekly mortgage rate (MORTGAGE30US) and the Case-Shiller home price index (CSUSHPINSA).

## Stocks and the risk-on, risk-off switch
In risk-on periods, investors chase growth. Stocks (especially tech), crypto, silver, copper, small caps and high-yield bonds tend to rise together, and safe havens like Treasuries, gold, the yen and the dollar lag. In risk-off periods, the reverse: money hides in Treasuries (yields fall), the dollar, the yen and often gold, while stocks and crypto fall. The VIX volatility index jumps during risk-off. One wrinkle: when the scare is inflation, as in 2022, stocks and bonds can fall together, because rising yields hurt both.

## A cheat sheet of common scenarios
Fed cuts rates and inflation is calm: yields and the dollar tend to fall. Gold, stocks, crypto and homebuilders tend to benefit. Mortgage rates ease. Inflation surprises high: yields rise, bonds fall, the dollar often rises, tech and crypto often drop, and gold is mixed. Recession fear: yields fall as people buy Treasuries, the dollar can rise, stocks, oil, silver and crypto usually fall, gold often holds up, and the gold-silver ratio rises. Strong growth with calm inflation: stocks, copper and silver tend to do well, yields drift up, and gold is often flat. War or financial crisis: gold, the dollar and Treasuries usually gain, at least at first, and risk assets sell off. Treat all of this as a starting framework, then check the live data. Each cycle has its own twist.

## Correlation is not a rule
A correlation measures how two things moved together in the past. It changes over time and can flip. Gold and real yields decoupled. Bitcoin sometimes trades with stocks and sometimes on its own. Stocks and bonds moved opposite each other for twenty years, then together in 2022. Before relying on a relationship, check it on a chart for the recent period, for example with TradingView's Compare button or the Correlation Coefficient indicator. Ask why it should hold now, and what would break it.
